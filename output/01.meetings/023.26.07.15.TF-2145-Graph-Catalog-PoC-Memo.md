## Graphs, Insights, and Agents (Oh my!)

For many years, our Terraform commercial customers have had problem statements that can essentially be boiled down to "I need you to give me insights about my cloud real estate and how Terraform works with it, at scale". Which workspaces consume outputs from which and what breaks if I change it? What exact provider versions are running across 500 workspaces right now? Before I touch this module, how many downstream workspaces will be affected? To combat some of these, in June 2023 we announced a feature called the Terraform Explorer. Bluntly, for several reasons - roadmap priority, staffing, acquisition complexity, etc etc - Terraform estates nonetheless continue to be opaque at scale. The Explorer feature is coupled tightly to the Atlas monolith, and is just now looking at resource and attribute-level data collection several years later. Explorer is the right direction, but as an R&D organization we've not made the progress we ought to have with it yet.

The Explorer feature at the time also navigated a contentious period where a bold new product offering was to be the source of truth for such insights: InfraGraph. Without falling into the deep rabbithole on InfraGraph lore (because that's not our purpose here), the path set forth was to try and essentially leave general resource data to IG while retaining HCP Terraform specific metadata (things like projects, workspaces, modules, etc) to "Explorer". InfraGraph went through its own pains, eventually diverging from the original idea of its architecture (utilizing the Terraform ecosystem and pluggable provider schemas) to its current-day reality of having its own bespoke Connector architecture completely independent of Terraform.

Years later, LLMs now are changing the software industry and making data and actions available to humans via NLP and by non-human operators via MCP and other model-friendly APIs is an increasingly frequent feature request. The Meridian project (now called "Intent-Driven Workflows for IBM Terraform for Z and LinuxONE") started as an incredibly broad and ambitious project to build 'The Agentic Datacenter' that has since narrowed to a still-ambitious project to introduce agentic change management via Terraform to Z customers.

In the opinion paper I wrote in March for IBM Think, I specifically mentioned what admittedly is the opposite strategy when it comes to agentic interfaces from Meridian:

> ...read-only introspection is the "low-hanging fruit" of the agentic era. Any initiative aimed at cloud visibility or resource mapping must treat the agent as the primary consumer. Instead of building shiny dashboards for human eyes, we build queryable, semantic "maps" of the infrastructure state. An agent should be able to traverse a complex graph of Terraform-managed resources to answer "What will break if I change this?" faster and more accurately than any human looking at a monitoring tab.

Change management is hard. Management of a complex system with complex states is hard enough, and throwing in a layer of non-determinism (AI) only exacerbates that difficulty. We should absolutely try to do it - but there's a lot to be learned from starting with better introspection of these complex estates where the stakes are lower (It's generally ok if your robot makes a mistake counting your EC2 instances. It's definitely not ok if your robot brings down the platform deleting a database).

Exploring insights of cloud real estates using an agentic interface? Sounds neat, we have a massive amount of work to do aligning the roadmaps of Terraform, InfraGraph, Meridian, etc, etc, right? Somebody call our reps, we're gonna need Neptune and unlimited tokens for Fable 5.

No. We do not.

Simply put, the core purpose of the "graph catalog" proof of concept is to draw attention to these sorts of points (my opinions, of course):

- Terraform has some longstanding, still-bafflingly-unsolved customer requirements that for years has been deferred to an "InfraGraph problem to solve" simply because the word 'graph' was uttered. That can't continue. Terraform has its own needs to keep its customers paying the bills. Perhaps someday its own 'graph' data can be exported to InfraGraph for IG's own broader vision, that's fine. But we need to stop 'avoiding progress' by convincing ourselves there's some scary overlap between product offerings.
- Terraform's 'graph' has always been a thing that everyone wants more out of, albeit a very misunderstood thing. A Terraform state does not inherently contain 'the graph'. 'The graph' is actually a set of graphs and combination of configuration (user intent) and state. It is a set of relationships walked at runtime during planning, every time.
- The Terraform Explorer concept is [still] good! ...but the progress/investment/priority of it is misaligned, several years later.
- You can have actually-really-useful-and-cool AI features in the Terraform platform that are very tractable and real which do not need a separate marketing campaign to realize value or even anything more ambitious than asking 'boring' query questions (to start!).
- We're really, really behind on the previous point. The fact that you cannot simply ask HCP Terraform "How many xlarge EC2 instances do I have across all the workspaces in my organization?" with a good visual is concerning. This is very basic functionality that I'd expect to have seen 3-6 months ago at this point.
- We're at risk of falling behind in this space. Without falling off topic, AI has lowered the barrier to entry for competitors to maintain their own Terraform runtimes which can adhere to the specification and bring interesting capabilities that actually are quite compelling. StateGraph is one example.

## Enter the "Terraform Graph Catalog"

Terraform Graph Catalog is a standalone Go service that maintains a queryable index of every Terraform resource instance, provider, and cross-workspace dependency across an HCP Terraform organization. It is an experiment I originally started as a project in the Terraform Frontiers group and as part of market investigation of StateGraph.

The service answers two related classes of questions:

- **Cross-workspace inventory** - "which workspaces use the AWS provider at version 5.x?", "how many S3 buckets does our org have?", "show me all resources in project X that have tag Y in their attributes."
- **Dependency impact** - "which resources depend on `aws_vpc.main`?", "if workspace `ws-network-prod` changes, which downstream workspaces are in its blast radius?"

A secondary capability is an LLM chat interface over the catalog, intended as a PoC of an agentic "ask anything about your infrastructure" experience. Additionally, it's an exploration into AI driving the UX of the product. As you ask queries, the MCP exposes the ability of the agent to 'highlight' visualizations in the UI for the user.

The name "Graph Catalog" is an intentionally clickbaity and temporary name to demonstrate that Terraform's existing v4 state representation and other artifacts are perfectly sufficient to provide the sort of value discussed above, and to do so in a way that progresses the architecture of the platform away from the monolithic Rails application and towards a more service-oriented future we've begun in efforts like the Janus initiative.

The name and even UX of the graph catalog PoC are not altogether that important. The rough architecture, ETL, and interfaces are. Call it 'Explorer V2'. Embed the UI in the existing Ember UI in Atlas if you like. None of that matters. In fact I'll even be disappointed if it's called 'graph catalog', that's kind of a terrible name.

A far more appropriate name? Explorer, maybe. Explorer Reloaded? Explorer II: The Insightening. Explorer Pro. Dora The Graph Explorer. I don't know man, I'm just an engineer.

## Relationship to Explorer

Explorer already exists in Atlas as a Rails engine that does cross-workspace resource and provider visibility. Understanding the difference between the two systems - and why Graph Catalog was built instead of extending Explorer - is the most important context for this handoff.

### What They Share

Both systems maintain a separate PostgreSQL database (and I even recommend any future 'graph catalog' service to use that very same 'Explorer' logical database) from Atlas's primary DB, populated by an asynchronous ingest pipeline triggered by Terraform run events. Both track resources, providers, and workspaces. Both expose a query API that Atlas (or its clients) call after establishing scope. Both are read-optimized denormalizations of data that Atlas already holds, designed to answer questions that we would like to isolate away from the main Atlas DB.

### Where They Diverge

The divergence is fundamental, not incidental:

| Dimension | Explorer | Graph Catalog |
|---|---|---|
| Deployment model | Rails engine embedded inside Atlas; same process, separate DB | Standalone Go service; entirely separate process and DB |
| Ingest mechanism | ActiveRecord after_commit hooks → Sidekiq jobs (7+ async hops) | Atlas / state-parser HTTP POST to ingest endpoints (2-3 hops) |
| What is stored per resource | Name, address, type, provider, module - no attribute values, no dependency graph | Full JSONB attribute values (sensitive values redacted) + full transitive dependency closure |
| Cross-workspace edges | Not modeled | Explicit `workspace_edges` table, sourced from `terraform_remote_state` data sources |
| Blast radius | Not possible | Native recursive CTE query across workspace edges |
| LLM interface | None | Tool-calling chat loop backed by OpenAI-compatible API |

Explorer was built to power the Explorer UI tab - a structured filter/sort/export interface for org operators. It reflects that constraint: it is optimized for human-readable table queries, not machine-readable dependency traversal. Graph Catalog was built with an agent as the primary consumer with a cute UI on top.

### What Explorer Has That Graph Catalog Does Not

Explorer tracks workspace-level operational metadata: current run status, drift counts, health check outcomes, VCS repo association, tags, and Terraform/Provider/Module versions. It also has bulk actions (lock N workspaces matching a filter), saved views, and CSV export. None of these are in Graph Catalog (but easily can be). The focus was on what Explorer doesn't have.

### What Graph Catalog Has That Explorer Does Not

Resource attribute values (e.g. `instance_type`, region, tag maps), full transitive dependency closures per resource instance, cross-workspace dependency edges, blast radius traversal, and the LLM chat layer.

### Recommendation on Coexistence

As they exist now, the two systems are complementary, not redundant. Explorer owns operational metadata and human-readable workspace reporting. Graph Catalog owns the dependency graph and attribute-level resource index. If anything, the team should consider whether Graph Catalog can eventually replace Explorer (or again, be Explorer V2) for workspace metadata (e.g., surfacing drift status in blast radius results) rather than re-ingesting that data separately.

## Architecture Overview

Graph Catalog follows the Janus extraction pattern: a standalone Go service owning its own Postgres database and Redis instance, communicating with the rest of the platform over HTTP with shared secrets. It has no gRPC surface yet (explicitly deferred).

Two secrets govern access:

- **Ingest secret** - used by `terraform-state-parser` to POST state data.
- **Service secret** - used by Atlas to POST plan data, PATCH provider versions, and create grants.

Client queries (resource lookups, blast radius, etc.) are authorized not by a secret but by a grant ID - a short-lived token that Atlas mints on behalf of a user after evaluating their permissions. The grant ID encodes which workspaces the caller is allowed to see.

The only extra data mechanism completely absent and added for the Catalog's sake that needed to be added from Atlas is the parsed plan, which in the PoC is just a Sidekiq job in Ruby. In a production setting a dispatch job in Go (a sibling to the state-parser) may be more appropriate.

---

## Data Model (High Level)

The schema is organized around four core concepts:

- **Resource instances** - one row per resource instance in the current state version of a workspace. Stores the full attribute JSON (with sensitive values redacted to the literal string `"<sensitive>"`), the full transitive dependency closure as a Postgres array, and basic metadata (type, module, provider). When a new state version is ingested, the entire workspace's rows are replaced atomically.
- **Resource configs** - plan-derived configuration metadata: which attributes reference which other resources (expression reference paths), and explicit `depends_on` declarations. This is separate from resource instances because plans are available before applies, and the config represents intent rather than actual state. This is what powers the `dependency_reason` annotation (whether a dependency is explicit, implicit through an attribute reference, or unknown).
- **Workspace providers** - provider configurations per workspace, sourced from plan JSON. Stores source, alias, version constraint, and eventually the exact resolved version from the lock file (patched separately after apply). Provider configuration values have sensitive expressions redacted on ingest by the catalog itself.
- **Workspace edges** - directed edges between workspaces: (consumer, producer) pairs derived from `terraform_remote_state` data sources. Atlas resolves workspace names to IDs before sending these. These edges are the foundation of blast radius traversal.
- **State outputs** - workspace root outputs, with sensitive output values nulled out.
- **Access grants** - described below under Access Control.

---

## Ingest Pipeline

Data enters Graph Catalog via three HTTP push endpoints, all POST/PATCH by Atlas or `terraform-state-parser`:

- **State ingest** (from `terraform-state-parser`, after apply) - delivers the full resource instance set for a workspace. The state parser pre-sanitizes sensitive attribute values before sending; Graph Catalog trusts this. The catalog replaces the entire workspace's resource and output rows in a single transaction, with an advisory lock preventing concurrent ingests for the same workspace. Ingest is idempotent: if the same state version ID is seen again, it is a no-op.
- **Plan ingest** (from Atlas, after plan) - delivers resource configs (expression references, `depends_on`), provider configurations, and resolved cross-workspace edges. The catalog itself redacts sensitive provider configuration values. Again, idempotent on plan ID.
- **Provider version patch** (from Atlas, after apply) - patches the exact resolved provider version onto existing provider rows, sourced from the `.terraform.lock.hcl` file. This is a separate call because the lock file data is only available at apply time, while plan data is available earlier.

What is notably absent: there is no polling, no event bus, no pub/sub. Graph Catalog is a pure push target. If a push is missed (service down, network error), the data is stale until the next successful push. The existing Atlas retry infrastructure (Sidekiq) would handle retries if the push calls are made from jobs; see Integration Work below.

## Access Control - The Grant System

Access control is the most architecturally distinctive part of Graph Catalog and worth understanding carefully.

Graph Catalog has no concept of organizations, projects, or users. It does not know who is asking or why, intentionally. Instead, Atlas evaluates permissions using its own Pundit/policy layer, then trades that authorization decision for a grant: a short-lived UUID that asserts "the holder of this token may query across this specific set of workspaces."

Grants are stored in two places simultaneously:

- **PostgreSQL** holds the workspace membership: which workspace IDs are in scope for a given grant ID. This is used at query time to scope every SQL query - no query can return data outside the grant's workspace set.
- **Redis** holds the grant's liveness: a key with a TTL. Redis is the authoritative signal for whether a grant is still valid. Every query checks Redis first; if the key is gone (expired or explicitly deleted), the grant is treated as nonexistent regardless of what Postgres says.

This split exists because Redis provides O(1) TTL-based expiry with no clock-skew issues, while Postgres provides the durable workspace membership list needed for SQL JOIN-time scoping. Together they give both fast authorization checks and exact access control at the data layer.

A background cleanup job runs every 15 minutes to delete expired grant rows from Postgres (Redis TTL handles the Redis side automatically).

## LLM Chat Feature

The service includes an LLM-backed chat interface, accessible via the dev UI, that allows natural language questions about the infrastructure catalog. It is built on a tool-calling loop: the service exposes a set of catalog query tools to an OpenAI-compatible model, which decides which tools to call and in what order to answer the user's question.

The tools fall into two categories:

- **Data tools** that query the catalog (`list_workspaces`, search resources by type/attribute, find dependents, get blast radius, list providers).
- **UI-action tools** that instruct the browser to do something visual (highlight a workspace, enter blast-radius mode, switch views).

The model is given a system prompt explaining the data model, the `<sensitive>` sentinel, and guidance on how to interpret queries. The loop runs up to six rounds of tool calls before returning a final answer. All of this is only available when the dev UI is enabled.

The LLM configuration (API URL, key, model name) is supplied via environment variables. Any OpenAI-compatible endpoint works.

---

## Dev UI

The dev UI is an embedded single-page application (vanilla JS + D3) served directly from the binary when `GRAPH_CATALOG_DEV_UI=true`. It provides three views:

- **Workspace graph**: nodes are workspaces, edges are cross-workspace dependencies. Intended as a visual sanity check of the ingested edge data.
- **Resource graph**: all resource instances in a selected workspace, laid out as a DAG by their dependency relationships.
- **Provider view**: a bipartite graph of providers and workspaces.

The graph renderer is sophisticated (three layout algorithms, color-coding by resource category, hover/focus interactions, blast-radius visual mode) and was ported from the "HCP InfraGraph" project. The chat panel is integrated into the UI sidebar.

The dev UI is explicitly not for production (currently). It is unauthenticated, scoped to internal tooling, and requires a flag to enable. It exists to make the PoC demonstrable without building a full Atlas integration.

## Integration Work Required

### Atlas (three integration points)

- **Plan ingest** - After every successful plan, Atlas needs to extract resource configuration, provider configuration, and cross-workspace edge data from the plan JSON and POST it to Graph Catalog. The tricky part is edge resolution: `terraform_remote_state` data sources reference workspaces by name, not ID, so Atlas must resolve names to external IDs before sending. Atlas already does this lookup for its own run pipeline; it needs to be plumbed through to a new ingest call.
- **Provider version patch** - After every apply, Atlas should send the resolved provider versions from the workspace's lock file. This is straightforward: the data is available in Atlas and the endpoint is simple.
- **Grant creation** - Any Atlas feature that wants to proxy catalog queries on behalf of a user needs to mint a grant first. This means: evaluate the user's workspace permissions (which Atlas already does), call `POST /api/v1/grants` with the allowed workspace IDs, get back a grant ID, and either return it to the client or use it to proxy queries server-side. The grant TTL should be set to the session lifetime or a short window appropriate to the use case.

All three of these are new Atlas code paths. Each call should be Sidekiq jobs for durable retries.

### terraform-state-parser

After each successful state parse, the state parser should POST the sanitized state to Graph Catalog's state ingest endpoint. The state parser already produces the sanitized resource list it delivers to Atlas; it needs an additional fanout to the catalog endpoint. The ingest secret (`GRAPH_CATALOG_INGEST_SECRET`) should be provisioned alongside the state parser's existing secrets.

## Production Readiness Concerns

### Structural / Architectural

- **Telemetry is a no-op.** The service has a metrics abstraction but it writes to a blackhole sink. There are no Datadog traces, no DogStatsd counters, no latency histograms. This needs to be wired up before production - a service with no observability is a service you cannot operate.
- **gRPC is absent.** The service speaks HTTP only. For the integration with Atlas this is fine in the short term, but the Janus pattern (which this service is modeled on) ultimately serves Atlas via gRPC. Whether Graph Catalog should follow suit depends on how Atlas ends up consuming it.
- **Redis is load-bearing for all queries.** A Redis outage takes down every query endpoint, not just the grant creation path. The team should decide whether a fallback - consulting Postgres `expires_at` when Redis is unreachable - is warranted, or whether Redis high availability is sufficient mitigation.
- **No rate limiting.** Ingest endpoints can be called at arbitrary rates. For a service that replaces entire workspace datasets in a transaction, a sufficiently large or fast ingest could cause Postgres contention.
- **Ingest does not scale to massive workspaces.** A workspace with tens of thousands of resources carrying full attribute JSON can produce payloads well beyond a single HTTP request. `PlanPayloadBuilder` in Atlas loads the entire plan JSON (potentially 500 MB+) into a Sidekiq worker's memory. Graph Catalog's ingest handlers perform a single DELETE + INSERT transaction that holds a lock proportional to workspace size. Chunked and/or streamed machinery is needed. The Archivist handoff approach Atlas already uses for state-parser is a reference model.

### Data Reliability

- **Missed pushes mean stale data.** Graph Catalog is a pure push target with no polling, no reconciliation loop, no backfill scheduler. This is a deliberate simplicity choice, but the integration layer must compensate with durable, retried delivery (Sidekiq jobs, not fire-and-forget).
- **Plan and state can get out of sync.** Plan data (configs, providers, edges) and state data (resource instances) are ingested independently. `dependency_reason` annotations may be stale or absent immediately after a workspace change or if an apply fails.

### Operational Gaps

- **No secrets rotation.** Both secrets are static env vars. Rotation requires a service restart.
- **No readiness/liveness split in health check.** The single `/healthz` endpoint returns 503 on any Postgres or Redis failure. Correct behavior may be to stay alive but mark unready.
- **Dev UI has no authentication.** When enabled, `/dev/graph` and `/dev/chat` expose the entire workspace graph with zero authentication. The `DEV_UI` flag must be treated as a hard production gate, not a feature toggle.
- **`make seed` is a stub.** No tooling to load synthetic data. Local development requires manually triggering ingest from the integrated services or writing a seed script.
- **The inference API is hand-waved.** I set up a local proxy to Copilot to demonstrate, but any agent feature in the product will need a proper inference API with limitations, rate controls, etc. For TFE, this may need to be a configurable interface for inference. This scope of work should not be taken lightly.

## Summary and Core "Must dos"

Graph Catalog is a well-structured PoC that needs several things to make a true production service: telemetry, production secrets management, a health model, rate limiting, and - most critically - the integration code in Atlas and `terraform-state-parser` that actually puts data into it.

It's a proof-of-concept. We do not need to call it 'graph catalog'. The plan parser (implemented as a Sidekiq job in Ruby) should probably be wholly replaced.

Core principles that were intentional and should be preserved:

1. **Scale is everything.** This feature must be able to handle thousands of workspaces and resources. Everything must be paginated, limited, and performant, tested beyond just a few `random_pets`. JPMC (our largest TFE customer) should be able to use this.
2. **Monolithic decomposition is a central principle.** Minimize the dependency on the monolith for this feature. It's very purposely its own service, its own database, with a clear ETL interface/handoff point where Terraform artifacts (state, config, etc) are provided. Authorization is 'grant'-style to explicitly leave Atlas as the source of truth and not replicate such things across services. Create explicit interfaces and don't skimp on them.
3. **The queryable state is/should be presumed as sanitized.** The core assumption is that a user config / provider schema setting the concept of 'sensitive' in Terraform means that sentinel values should be placed at parse time such that all sensitive values are never present in the graph catalog. Any LLM should not be given any access to such values. There's an inherent acceptance on the user's part that use of this feature means they accept this notion.

**Remember: This is an AI-assisted PoC. I steered a model for less than two days building it. There's good parts, but very likely bad ones too. Be critical and discerning of its current state.**
